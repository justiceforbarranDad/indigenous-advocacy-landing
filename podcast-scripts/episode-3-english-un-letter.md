# Justice for Barran: The UN Letter - English Podcast Script (20 minutes)

**INTRO (1 minute)**

Hello, and welcome to Justice for Barran. I'm Robert McGovern, and I'm speaking to you today about a case that has consumed five years of my family's life—a case that reveals how institutions in Canada systematically fail Indigenous children, even when those children are victims of attempted murder.

On February 14, 2021—early in the COVID-19 pandemic, when the world was locked down—my then-14-year-old son Barran was stabbed three times. In the left lung. In the chest. In the back. An iPhone in his jacket pocket deflected two additional stab attempts that doctors said would likely have been fatal.

He survived. But what happened after—what the government did, or rather what it refused to do—is the real tragedy.

Our family is multicultural: French, Irish, Canadian, Haitian, Aboriginal, and Italian. We are 11 in our immediate family, 25 in total with aunts, uncles, and cousins. We believe in collective care—in family supporting family through crisis. But during COVID, our extended family couldn't reach us. Borders were closed. Travel was restricted. The very relatives who would have rushed to Barran's side were locked out of the country.

Our Haitian family members—they came to Canada seeking safety from the tragedies in their homeland. Gang violence. Kidnappings. Political instability. Natural disasters. They came believing Canada would be different. They came believing their children would be safe. But watching Barran abandoned by Canadian institutions, they recognize something terrifying: the same systemic failure, the same institutional abandonment they fled in Haiti is happening here. They see the same patterns. The same silence. The same refusal to protect vulnerable children.

They are experiencing compounded trauma. Trauma from Haiti. Trauma from watching their extended family be abandoned in Canada. Trauma from realizing that safety was a false promise.

That's when government had a duty to step in. That's when institutions should have protected our son. Instead, they abandoned him—and in doing so, they abandoned the hope that brought our Haitian family to Canada in the first place.

Today, I want to share with you a letter I sent to the United Nations. A letter that documents five years of institutional failure, political intimidation, and systemic racism. A letter that proves that when an Indigenous child asks for help in Canada, the answer is often silence.

---

**PART 1: THE INCIDENT (2 minutes)**

February 14, 2021. Valentine's Day. A day that should have been about love became a day about survival.

Barran was 14 years old. Five feet ten inches tall. He weighed 300 pounds. He lived with Type 2 diabetes and a documented mobility disability—conditions that are tragically common among Indigenous youth in Canada because of intergenerational poverty and colonial healthcare deprivation. He is part of a family that spans continents and cultures: Hatchet Lake Dene First Nation heritage, Irish roots, Haitian family, Italian heritage. We are a multicultural family that values collective care, community support, and family unity. Our Haitian relatives understand family obligation—they've lived through crises in Haiti where family is all you have. Our Indigenous relatives understand collective responsibility—it's woven into our culture. Our Irish and Italian relatives understand loyalty and protection of the vulnerable.

But during the pandemic, we were separated. Isolated. Unable to be there for each other in the way our cultures teach us to be. Our Haitian family members—many of whom came to Canada to escape institutional failure—were locked out by borders. They couldn't be there. They couldn't help. They could only watch from a distance as Canadian institutions failed our son in the same way Haitian institutions had failed them.

He was stabbed three times in Montreal. The perpetrator was never convicted. He remains at large to this day.

Barran was hospitalized for six days at the Montreal Children's Hospital. He had a chest tube for three of those days. Three people were at the scene of the attack. All three were denied recognition as victims. All three were denied services.

When Barran left the hospital, his first words were: I miss my siblings. I miss Grandma. I miss my cousins.

Not I'm in pain. Not I'm scared. His first thought was about his family—the aunts, uncles, and cousins who couldn't reach him because of COVID lockdowns. He wanted his extended family. He wanted the collective care that our multicultural heritage teaches us. He wanted to feel held by his people. Our Haitian grandmother wanted to be there. Our Haitian aunts wanted to be there. They know what it means to lose a child to institutional failure. They came to Canada to escape that. But they couldn't reach him.

That tells you something about this child. That tells you about his character. That tells you about what we had to fight for over the next five years—alone, isolated, without the family support that should have been there. And it tells you about the compounded trauma our Haitian family members carry—the realization that the institutional abandonment they fled in Haiti has followed them to Canada.

---

**PART 2: THE DENIAL OF SERVICES (3 minutes)**

In April 2021, just two months after the stabbing, I formally requested psychological services for Barran. I contacted the Direction de la protection de la jeunesse—the DPJ—the Quebec child protection agency. I contacted the Centre intégré de santé et de services sociaux—the CISSS—the integrated health and social services center.

Both refused.

Not a single psychological session was provided to my son in the months following his near-fatal stabbing. Not one.

The Quebec Ombudsman—the Protecteur du Citoyen—later confirmed in writing that the DPJ had failed in its legal duty of care. Despite this official finding, no corrective action was taken. No services were ever provided.

Then came Jordan's Principle. Many of you may not know what this is. Jordan's Principle is a federal legal obligation of Canada. It was established specifically to ensure that First Nations children receive government services without delay, without denial, and without jurisdictional disputes being used as a barrier.

We discovered this policy ourselves through our own research. The government never told us about it. When I requested that Barran's file be transferred to the Montreal Native Friendship Centre—to access culturally appropriate services, to work with a social worker familiar with Indigenous families—the DPJ refused.

In January 2023, the DPJ withdrew entirely from Barran's file. No transition plan. No referral to alternative services. No follow-up of any kind.

Following this withdrawal, Barran's access to education collapsed. His mental health deteriorated severely. He is now 18 years old. He receives approximately $300 per month in social assistance. He has been unable to work or attend school as a direct result of five years of institutional failure and untreated trauma.

---

**PART 3: THE CIRCLE OF INSTITUTIONAL OBSTRUCTION (5 minutes)**

Over five years, we sought help from every institution available to us. Every single one refused, redirected, or dismissed. This closed circle is itself evidence of systemic failure.

Let me walk you through it.

The DPJ and CISSS Laval refused psychological services in April 2021. They refused cultural transfer. They withdrew in January 2023 with no follow-up. The Quebec Ombudsman confirmed their failure in writing.

The CDPDJ—the Quebec Human Rights Commission—received a systemic racism complaint filed by CRARR in April 2024. They dismissed it in 2025 using the DPJ immunity clause under the Youth Protection Act. The commission ruled it could not prove "bad faith," rendering systemic racism legally unchallengeable. Think about that. Systemic racism is legally unchallengeable in Quebec.

I contacted all 125 Members of the Quebec National Assembly. All 125. Only one responded. The MNA for Laval-des-Rapides, Céline Haytayan, sent a written communication threatening criminal harassment charges against our family for continuing to ask questions about DPJ conduct.

I contacted the Quebec Ethics Commissioner. Their response: no mechanism exists for citizens to file complaints against MNAs. All processes are confidential. No disclosure is provided to complainants.

The Quebec Ombudsman confirmed DPJ failure in writing. No enforceable corrective action resulted.

IVAC, the victim compensation program, retroactively changed its legislation in a way that reduced Barran's eligibility for compensation.

I filed a complaint with the Canadian Human Rights Commission. Their response: this matter appears to be provincial. We're referring you to the CDPDJ—which had already refused.

I contacted federal MP Annie Koutrakis. Jordan's Principle is a federal obligation. Her office responded with a generic automated email. The federal obligation was not addressed.

I contacted the Minister of Justice Sean Fraser directly. No substantive response.The Senate of Canada was contacted. Senator Patrick Brazeau—an Indigenous senator was reached out to. Senator Michele Audette—the former co-commissioner of the Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls National Inquiry was contacted. Senators Boisvenu and Bellemare were contacted. No substantive assistance from any of them.

Then came the Governor General of Canada. In late February 2026, after exhausting every other option—every provincial ministry, every federal MP, every senator—a call was made to the office of Mary Simon, the Governor General of Canada. The request was simple: What do we do next? Where do we go when every institution has failed us?

The response was a wellness check.

Not to Barran. Not to the two Aboriginal children in the family who actually needed help. A wellness check was sent to the parent—to the person who had been fighting for five years to get their child help.

Then another wellness check came. And another. And another. Four wellness checks in one month. Four times, authorities showed up at the door—not to help the children, but seemingly to intimidate the parent who had dared to ask the Governor General's office for assistance.

This is institutional retaliation. This is what happens when you exhaust every legal avenue and then ask the highest office in Canada for help. Instead of assistance, you receive intimidation. Instead of services for your children, you receive wellness checks targeting you.

The Governor General's office—the office of the Crown's representative in Canada—participated in silencing this family. Not through direct refusal, but through the weaponization of mental health services as a tool of intimidation.

Let that sink in. The highest office in Canada responded to a family's desperate plea for help by sending wellness checks to intimidate the parent seeking assistance.

The Assembly of First Nations was contacted in 2021. They ghosted the family. For four years. Complete silence. No response. No acknowledgment. Nothing. Until today—March 30, 2026—when they finally reached out. Four years of silence from the national organization that is supposed to represent First Nations children and families.

And here's what makes this even more striking: As of late March 2026, following the April 2025 federal election, a record number of Indigenous politicians now sit in the Canadian Parliament. At least 12 Indigenous Members of Parliament were elected, including Mandy Gull-Masty, Rebecca Chartrand, and Wade Grant. Other notable Indigenous MPs include Leah Gazan, Lori Idlout, Jaime Battiste, and Billy Morin. The Senate also includes several Indigenous Senators, such as Patrick Brazeau, Michèle Audette, and Paul Prosper.

So we have unprecedented Indigenous representation at the highest levels of Canadian government. And yet, this child—an Indigenous victim of attempted murder—was still abandoned. His case was still ignored. His family was still threatened with criminal charges for asking for help.

What does that tell you about the depth of systemic failure? When Indigenous politicians themselves cannot or will not intervene to protect an Indigenous child from institutional abandonment, we are looking at something far more profound than simple racism. We are looking at a system so broken that even those with power to change it have chosen silence.

APTN—Aboriginal Peoples Television Network—produced and broadcast a news report documenting Barran's case. The report was subsequently removed. We were given no explanation. Even the media that covers Indigenous issues chose silence over accountability.

This pattern—in which every institution refers to another institution that has already refused—is the operational mechanism of systemic racism. No single actor needs to express racist intent. The architecture of the system guarantees that Indigenous families cannot obtain relief regardless of how persistently or lawfully they seek it.

---

**PART 4: POLITICAL INTIMIDATION (2 minutes)**

In November 2023, the office of MNA Céline Haytayan sent our family a written communication. It stated that any further contact regarding the DPJ or related government agency conduct "could be considered criminal harassment" and that police charges "could be filed."

This threat was issued in response to our family's repeated, polite, and entirely lawful requests for information about why our Indigenous son was denied services following an attempt on his life.

Let me be clear: we were threatened with criminal charges for asking questions. For seeking accountability. For asking why our son was abandoned.

The use of criminal harassment threats by an elected official to silence a victim's family violates freedom of expression. It violates the right to petition government. It violates the right to an effective remedy—rights guaranteed under the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and under international human rights law.

The APTN news report documenting Barran's case was broadcast and then removed. No explanation was given. The suppression of media coverage of Indigenous rights violations is a matter of serious concern.

---

**PART 5: THE INTERNATIONAL FRAMEWORK (3 minutes)**

This is why we have turned to the United Nations. Because Canada has made commitments to the world. Commitments that it is violating in Barran's case.

The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples guarantees the right to life, physical integrity, health, and social security of Indigenous persons. It calls for special measures for the protection of Indigenous children. Barran has none of these.

The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child guarantees disabled children the right to special care and assistance. It guarantees the right to the highest attainable standard of health. It guarantees the right to recovery and reintegration for child victims of violence. Barran has received none of these.

Jordan's Principle is a Canadian federal legal obligation. It is a non-discretionary duty to provide services to First Nations children without delay or jurisdictional dispute. It has been violated in its entirety in Barran's case.

The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights guarantees non-discrimination and equal protection before the law. Barran has received neither.

The Canadian Victims Bill of Rights guarantees victims the right to information, protection, participation, and restitution. All of these have been denied to Barran.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission issued Calls to Action. Specific obligations regarding Indigenous child welfare, access to services, and family support. Each remains unimplemented in Barran's case.

In 2025, the CDPDJ itself issued a systemic inquiry into Nunavik. It confirmed the identical pattern: under-resourced, culturally inappropriate, and harmful interventions toward Indigenous children across Quebec.

---

**PART 6: WHAT WE'RE ASKING FOR (2 minutes)**

We have asked the United Nations to issue an urgent communication to the Government of Canada requesting an immediate explanation of the failure to apply Jordan's Principle in Barran's case and the systemic barriers that caused this family to be denied services for over five years.

We are asking Canada to ensure that Barran receives immediate psychological, medical, and social services appropriate to the severity of his trauma, his documented disabilities, and his status as a First Nations victim of attempted murder.

We are asking Canada to investigate and report on the use of the DPJ immunity clause under the Youth Protection Act as a mechanism that shields systemic racial discrimination from human rights accountability.

We are asking Canada to address the written use of criminal harassment threats by an elected official to silence a victim's family, as a violation of the right to freedom of expression and the right to petition government.

We are asking Canada to conduct a formal audit of Jordan's Principle implementation in Quebec, with specific focus on First Nations youth who are victims of serious crime.

We are asking that Canada make public any communications arising from this case, so that our family may be informed of Canada's response and hold it to account.

---

**OUTRO (1 minute)**

Five years. Five years of asking for help. Five years of institutional silence. Five years of watching my son suffer because the government of Canada decided that an Indigenous child was not worth helping.

We believe strongly that justice will come. We ask only that the world bear witness to what has been done to our son and to our family, and that institutions use the authority entrusted to them to hold Canada accountable to the obligations it has made to the world.

This is Justice for Barran. Thank you for listening.
