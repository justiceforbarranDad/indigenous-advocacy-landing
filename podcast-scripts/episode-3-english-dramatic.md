# Episode 3: Justice for Barran - The UN Letter (Dramatic Hybrid Version)

## DRAMATIC OPENING - THE MOMENT

**[Sound: Quiet, then a sudden sharp sound - a knife sound effect]**

Valentine's Day, 2021. Montreal. A winter night. A 14-year-old boy. Three stab wounds.

Left lung. Chest. Back.

An iPhone in his jacket pocket. Two more stab attempts. The phone deflected them. The doctors said those two deflected wounds would have been fatal.

He survived.

But survival was only the beginning of the tragedy.

His name is Barran. And this is his story. A story that reveals how a nation can systematically abandon its most vulnerable children—even when those children are victims of attempted murder. Even when they are Indigenous.

---

## PART ONE: THE FAMILY - MULTICULTURAL, MULTICONTINENTAL

This family is not simple. It cannot be reduced to a single narrative.

They are French. They are Irish. They are Canadian. They are Haitian. They are Aboriginal. They are Italian.

Eleven people in the immediate family. Twenty-five in total—aunts, uncles, cousins spread across continents and cultures.

The Haitian members came to Canada seeking safety. They fled gang violence. They fled kidnappings. They fled political instability. They fled natural disasters. They came believing Canada would be different. They came believing their children would be safe.

But in 2021, during the COVID-19 pandemic, when borders were closed and travel was restricted, they watched something terrible unfold. They watched from a distance as their extended family—the family they came to Canada to protect—was systematically abandoned by Canadian institutions.

The Haitian grandmother wanted to be there. The Haitian aunts wanted to be there. They know what it means to lose a child to institutional failure. They lived it in Haiti. They came to Canada to escape it.

But they couldn't reach him. The borders were closed. The pandemic had locked them out.

So they watched. From a distance. As the same institutional abandonment they fled in Haiti followed them to Canada.

This is compounded trauma. This is intergenerational betrayal.

---

## PART TWO: THE IMMEDIATE AFTERMATH - SILENCE

April 2021. Two months after the stabbing.

Barran needed help. Psychological services were formally requested. The family reached out to the Direction de la protection de la jeunesse—the DPJ—Quebec's child protection agency.

Both the DPJ and the integrated health services center refused.

Not delayed. Not pending. Refused.

Not a single psychological session. Not one. For a child who had been stabbed three times and was clearly traumatized.

The Quebec Ombudsman later confirmed in writing that the DPJ had violated its legal duty of care.

But no corrective action was taken. No services were provided. The confirmation of failure meant nothing.

---

## PART THREE: JORDAN'S PRINCIPLE - THE FEDERAL OBLIGATION

Then the family discovered Jordan's Principle.

For those who don't know: Jordan's Principle is a federal legal obligation of Canada. It was established to ensure that First Nations children receive government services without delay, without denial, and without jurisdictional disputes being used as an excuse.

A simple principle. A child's right to services. No bureaucratic games. No passing the buck between provinces and federal government.

The family requested that Barran's file be transferred to the Montreal Native Friendship Centre—to access culturally appropriate services, to work with a social worker familiar with Indigenous families.

The DPJ refused.

In January 2023—two years after the stabbing—the DPJ withdrew entirely from Barran's file. No transition plan. No referral to alternative services. No follow-up.

Barran's access to education collapsed. His mental health deteriorated severely.

He is now 18 years old. He receives approximately 300 dollars per month in social assistance. He has been unable to work or attend school.

Five years of institutional failure. Five years of untreated trauma.

---

## PART FOUR: THE INSTITUTIONAL MAZE - EVERY DOOR CLOSED

Over five years, help was sought from every institution available.

All 125 Members of the Quebec National Assembly were contacted. All 125.

Only one responded. The MNA for Laval-des-Rapides sent a written communication threatening criminal harassment charges for continuing to ask questions about DPJ conduct.

The family was threatened with criminal charges for asking why their child was abandoned.

Federal MP Annie Koutrakis was contacted. Jordan's Principle is a federal obligation. Her office responded with a generic automated email.

The Minister of Justice was contacted directly. No substantive response.

Senators were contacted—Patrick Brazeau, an Indigenous senator. Michele Audette, the former co-commissioner of the Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls National Inquiry.

No substantive assistance.

The Canadian Human Rights Commission was contacted. Their response: this is provincial. We're referring you to the CDPDJ—which had already refused.

Every institution referred to another institution that had already refused.

This is the operational mechanism of systemic racism. No single actor needs to express racist intent. The architecture of the system guarantees that Indigenous families cannot obtain relief.

---

## PART FIVE: THE ASSEMBLY OF FIRST NATIONS - FOUR YEARS OF SILENCE

The Assembly of First Nations—the national organization supposed to represent First Nations children and families—was contacted in 2021.

They ghosted the family.

For four years. Complete silence. No response. No acknowledgment. Nothing.

Until today—March 30, 2026—when they finally reached out.

Four years.

---

## PART SIX: THE GOVERNOR GENERAL - RETALIATION

In late February 2026, after exhausting every other option—every provincial ministry, every federal MP, every senator, every national organization—a call was made to the office of Mary Simon, the Governor General of Canada.

The request was simple: What do we do next? Where do we go when every institution has failed us?

The response was a wellness check.

Not to Barran. Not to the two Aboriginal children in the family who actually needed help.

A wellness check was sent to the parent—to the person who had been fighting for five years to get their child help.

Then another wellness check came. And another. And another.

Four wellness checks in one month.

Four times, authorities showed up at the door—not to help the children, but seemingly to intimidate the parent who had dared to ask the Governor General's office for assistance.

This is institutional retaliation. This is what happens when you exhaust every legal avenue and then ask the highest office in Canada for help.

Instead of assistance, you receive intimidation.

Instead of services for your children, you receive wellness checks targeting you.

The Governor General's office—the office of the Crown's representative in Canada—participated in silencing this family. Not through direct refusal, but through the weaponization of mental health services as a tool of intimidation.

The highest office in Canada responded to a family's desperate plea for help by sending wellness checks to intimidate the parent seeking assistance.

---

## PART SEVEN: UNPRECEDENTED REPRESENTATION - AND STILL ABANDONED

As of late March 2026, following the April 2025 federal election, a record number of Indigenous politicians now sit in the Canadian Parliament.

At least 12 Indigenous Members of Parliament were elected, including Mandy Gull-Masty, Rebecca Chartrand, and Wade Grant. Other notable Indigenous MPs include Leah Gazan, Lori Idlout, Jaime Battiste, and Billy Morin.

The Senate also includes several Indigenous Senators, such as Patrick Brazeau, Michele Audette, and Paul Prosper.

Unprecedented Indigenous representation at the highest levels of Canadian government.

And yet, this child—an Indigenous victim of attempted murder—was still abandoned.

His case was still ignored.

His family was still threatened with criminal charges for asking for help.

His family was still intimidated with wellness checks by the Governor General's office for seeking assistance.

When Indigenous politicians themselves cannot or will not intervene to protect an Indigenous child from institutional abandonment, when the Governor General's office weaponizes mental health services against a desperate family, we are looking at something far more profound than simple racism.

We are looking at a system so broken that even those with power to change it have chosen silence and intimidation.

---

## PART EIGHT: THE VIOLATIONS - INTERNATIONAL COMMITMENTS

This is why we have turned to the United Nations.

Because Canada has made commitments to the world. Commitments that it is violating in this case.

The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples guarantees the right to life, physical integrity, health, and social security of Indigenous persons. It calls for special measures for the protection of Indigenous children.

This child has none of these.

The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child guarantees disabled children the right to special care and assistance. It guarantees the right to the highest attainable standard of health. It guarantees the right to recovery and reintegration for child victims of violence.

This child has received none of these.

Jordan's Principle is a Canadian federal legal obligation. It is a non-discretionary duty to provide services to First Nations children without delay or jurisdictional dispute.

It has been violated in its entirety in this case.

The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights guarantees non-discrimination and equal protection before the law.

This child has received neither.

The Canadian Victims Bill of Rights guarantees victims the right to information, protection, participation, and restitution.

All of these have been denied.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission issued Calls to Action. Specific obligations regarding Indigenous child welfare, access to services, and family support.

Each remains unimplemented in this case.

---

## PART NINE: THE CALL TO ACTION

We have asked the United Nations to issue an urgent communication to the Government of Canada requesting an immediate explanation of the failure to apply Jordan's Principle in this case.

We are asking Canada to ensure that this child receives immediate psychological, medical, and social services appropriate to the severity of his trauma, his documented disabilities, and his status as a First Nations victim of attempted murder.

We are asking Canada to investigate the weaponization of wellness checks by the Governor General's office as a tool of intimidation against a family seeking help.

We are asking Canada to conduct a formal audit of Jordan's Principle implementation in Quebec, with specific focus on First Nations youth who are victims of serious crime.

We are asking that Canada make public any communications arising from this case, so that this family may be informed of Canada's response and hold it to account.

---

## CLOSING - THE RECKONING

Five years. Five years of asking for help. Five years of institutional silence. Five years of watching a child suffer because the government of Canada decided that an Indigenous child was not worth helping.

Five years of intimidation, retaliation, and weaponization of government services against a desperate family.

But something is changing.

The world is watching now.

The United Nations is listening.

And the reckoning is coming.

We believe strongly that justice will come. We ask only that the world bear witness to what has been done to this child and to this family, and that institutions use the authority entrusted to them to hold Canada accountable to the obligations it has made to the world.

This is Justice for Barran.

Thank you for listening.
