# Episode 2: Barran's Story - Haitian Family in Canada (Not Better)
## English Script (4 Speakers, ~50 minutes)

**INTRO MUSIC:** Haitian drums and strings (15 seconds)

---

### SPEAKER INTRODUCTIONS

**SPEAKER 1 (Legal/Justice - Male):** Welcome back to Justice for Barran. I'm [Speaker 1], a legal advocate focused on systemic justice and human rights.

**SPEAKER 2 (Community/Healing - Female):** I'm [Speaker 2], a community organizer working with diaspora families.

**SPEAKER 3 (Diaspora/Advocacy - Male):** I'm [Speaker 3], a diaspora activist focused on connecting communities across borders.

**SPEAKER 4 (Medical/Health - Female):** I'm [Speaker 4], a healthcare advocate working with marginalized communities. Today we're diving deep into Barran's story and exploring how it connects to other Haitian children's experiences in Canada.

---

### SEGMENT 1: THE PROMISE VS. THE REALITY (8 minutes)

**SPEAKER 3:** When Haitian families come to Canada, they're told: "This is better. This is safe. Your children will have opportunities." That's the promise. That's what keeps families going through the immigration process, the paperwork, the uncertainty.

**SPEAKER 2:** But the reality is very different. Haitian families arrive in Canada and face immediate barriers—language, discrimination, poverty, institutional racism. And when children are involved, those barriers become life-threatening.

**SPEAKER 1:** We see this pattern repeatedly. A Haitian family arrives in Quebec. They're poor. They're navigating systems in a language that's not their first language. They're sending remittances to Haiti. And then something happens—a child gets sick, a teenager has a crisis, family trauma emerges—and instead of getting help, they get the DPJ.

**SPEAKER 4:** And here's what's critical: the medical system fails them first. A Haitian child presents with symptoms—trauma responses, behavioral issues, mental health crisis. The doctor doesn't understand the cultural context. They don't speak Creole. They don't ask about family history, immigration trauma, systemic racism. They see "poor Haitian kid" and make assumptions.

**SPEAKER 2:** Those assumptions then get reported to the DPJ. And the DPJ, which is also systemically racist and culturally incompetent, sees "poor Haitian family" and assumes abuse or neglect. The system is designed to fail Haitian families.

---

### SEGMENT 2: BARRAN'S STORY - THE TRAUMA (12 minutes)

**SPEAKER 1:** Let's talk about Barran specifically. Since 2021, this family has been seeking help after serious trauma. A teenager stabbed three times. Three times. That's not a minor incident; that's a major trauma event that requires comprehensive support.

**SPEAKER 2:** And the family did everything right. They sought help. They went to the DPJ. They engaged with systems. They were trying to protect their child and get mental health support. But instead of getting help, they got abandoned.

**SPEAKER 3:** The DPJ became involved in 2021. For two years, there was some level of engagement. But in January 2023, the DPJ withdrew. No adequate follow-up. No education support. No mental health services. Just gone. And the family was left with a traumatized teenager, no support, no answers.

**SPEAKER 4:** From a medical perspective, this is catastrophic. A teenager who's been stabbed three times needs comprehensive trauma care. They need mental health support, physical rehabilitation, social support. They need their family supported so they can provide emotional safety. Instead, they got nothing.

**SPEAKER 1:** And here's where Jordan's Principle comes in. Jordan's Principle guarantees that First Nations children receive no-delay services for health and social needs. It's a federal commitment. It's the law. But it was completely ignored in Barran's case.

**SPEAKER 2:** So you have a Haitian-Irish-Indigenous child who's been traumatized, whose family is trying to get help, and the system fails them at every level. The medical system fails them. The DPJ fails them. Jordan's Principle is ignored. The family is left in crisis.

---

### SEGMENT 3: OTHER HAITIAN CHILDREN IN CANADA (12 minutes)

**SPEAKER 3:** And Barran is not alone. This is happening to other Haitian children across Canada. We don't have exact numbers because the system doesn't track this data, but we know from community reports that Haitian children are disproportionately represented in the child protection system.

**SPEAKER 2:** I've worked with families in Montreal, Laval, Ottawa, Gatineau. The stories are remarkably similar. A Haitian family arrives. They're poor. They're navigating systems. Something happens—trauma, illness, crisis. Instead of getting help, they get the DPJ.

**SPEAKER 1:** One case I worked on: a Haitian mother, single parent, working two jobs to support her three children and send money to her mother in Haiti. One of her children got sick—serious illness requiring hospitalization. While the child was hospitalized, the mother couldn't work. She fell behind on rent. The landlord reported her to the DPJ for "housing instability." The DPJ showed up, saw poverty, and opened an investigation.

**SPEAKER 4:** This is medical system failure compounded by DPJ failure. The child gets sick—which could happen to any family. But because the family is Haitian and poor, poverty becomes grounds for child protection intervention. The system doesn't see a sick child; it sees a "neglectful family."

**SPEAKER 3:** Another case: a Haitian teenager, immigrant, dealing with trauma from migration and systemic racism. The teenager has behavioral issues at school. The school reports to the DPJ. The DPJ gets involved. Instead of addressing the trauma, the system criminalizes the family.

**SPEAKER 2:** And the pattern is always the same: poverty is criminalized, cultural differences are pathologized, and Haitian families are blamed for systemic failures. The system says "you're not parenting right" instead of asking "what support does this family need?"

---

### SEGMENT 4: THE SILENCE - POLITICAL ABANDONMENT (10 minutes)

**SPEAKER 1:** Now here's what makes Barran's case particularly significant. The family didn't just accept the system's failure. They tried to get help. They contacted elected officials. At every level—municipal, provincial, federal. They reached out to more than 50 elected representatives.

**SPEAKER 3:** And the response? Silence. Complete silence. Not one elected official responded with meaningful help. Not one. And when the family continued to advocate, they got a cease-and-desist letter from their MNA threatening criminal harassment charges if they continued to contact her office.

**SPEAKER 2:** This is what happens when you're a Haitian family trying to get justice. You're not just failed by the DPJ; you're silenced by politicians. The system doesn't want to hear from you. It doesn't want to be held accountable.

**SPEAKER 4:** And this has health consequences. The family is in crisis, the child is traumatized, and instead of getting support, they're being threatened. The stress, the anxiety, the sense of hopelessness—this is medical trauma. This is what institutional abandonment does to families.

**SPEAKER 1:** But here's what's powerful: the family didn't stop. They kept advocating. They documented everything. They built a case. And now they're speaking publicly. They're telling Barran's story. They're building international family solidarity.

---

### SEGMENT 5: HAITIAN FAMILIES DURING COVID (10 minutes)

**SPEAKER 3:** And we can't talk about Haitian families in Canada without talking about COVID. The lockdowns created a perfect storm for diaspora families.

**SPEAKER 2:** During COVID, Haitian families in Laval and across Quebec were completely isolated. They couldn't visit extended family. They couldn't go to church. They couldn't access community support. And many lost their jobs or had their hours cut.

**SPEAKER 1:** At the same time, the DPJ was still operating. In fact, lockdowns made families more vulnerable to DPJ intervention. More home visits. More opportunities to see "poverty" and open investigations. Haitian families were targeted during the most vulnerable period.

**SPEAKER 4:** From a medical perspective, COVID was devastating for Haitian communities. Healthcare access collapsed. Mental health services were unavailable. Medications were unaffordable. And Haitian patients faced racism in the healthcare system—doctors refusing to treat them, dismissing their symptoms, blaming their poverty for their health problems.

**SPEAKER 3:** And the remittance crisis. Haitian families in Canada couldn't send money to Haiti. Families in Haiti were starving. And families in Laval were facing impossible choices: help family here or help family in Haiti? How do you choose?

**SPEAKER 2:** This is what diaspora consciousness means during crisis. You're locked down in Canada, isolated from your community, losing your income, facing DPJ investigation. And your mother is starving in Haiti. You can't help. You can't do anything. The helplessness is overwhelming.

---

### SEGMENT 6: WHAT JUSTICE LOOKS LIKE (8 minutes)

**SPEAKER 1:** So what does justice look like for Barran? For other Haitian children? For diaspora families?

**SPEAKER 2:** First, it means reforming the DPJ. It means cultural competency training. It means hiring Creole-speaking social workers. It means stopping the criminalization of poverty. It means recognizing that Haitian parenting practices are not abuse.

**SPEAKER 3:** Second, it means political accountability. It means elected officials responding to constituents. It means taking Haitian families seriously. It means investigating why Haitian children are disproportionately in the child protection system.

**SPEAKER 4:** Third, it means medical system reform. It means Creole-speaking healthcare providers. It means culturally appropriate mental health services. It means affordable medications. It means recognizing that Haitian patients have specific health needs related to migration trauma and systemic racism.

**SPEAKER 1:** And finally, it means international family solidarity. It means Haitian families connecting with Indigenous families, with other marginalized communities. It means recognizing that Barran's story is part of a larger story of systemic oppression and collective resistance.

**SPEAKER 2:** Justice for Barran is not just about one family. It's about transforming systems so that no Haitian family has to experience what Barran's family has experienced.
