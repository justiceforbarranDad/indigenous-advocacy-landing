# Episode 6: International Family Power - Diaspora Building Legacy
## English Script (4 Speakers, ~55 minutes)

**INTRO MUSIC:** Haitian drums and strings (15 seconds)

---

### SPEAKER INTRODUCTIONS

**SPEAKER 1 (Legal/Justice - Male):** Welcome to the final episode: International Family Power - Diaspora Building Legacy. I'm [Speaker 1], a legal advocate focused on systemic justice.

**SPEAKER 2 (Community/Healing - Female):** I'm [Speaker 2], a community organizer.

**SPEAKER 3 (Diaspora/Advocacy - Male):** I'm [Speaker 3], a diaspora activist.

**SPEAKER 4 (Medical/Health - Female):** I'm [Speaker 4], a healthcare advocate. Today we're exploring how diaspora families are building collective power and creating lasting change.

---

### SEGMENT 1: FROM INDIVIDUAL TRAUMA TO COLLECTIVE POWER (10 minutes)

**SPEAKER 3:** Throughout this series, we've talked about Barran's story. We've talked about systemic failures. We've talked about trauma. But what's powerful is that this family didn't stay silent. They didn't accept the system's failures. They organized. They built power.

**SPEAKER 2:** And that's what this final episode is about. It's about how individual trauma becomes collective power. It's about how one family's story becomes a movement. It's about diaspora families building international solidarity.

**SPEAKER 1:** Barran's family did something remarkable. They documented the silence. They told their story publicly. They connected with other families. They built a case for systemic change. They created Justice for Barran.

**SPEAKER 4:** And in doing that, they created a model for other diaspora families. They showed that it's possible to resist. It's possible to demand accountability. It's possible to build power.

**SPEAKER 3:** This is what diaspora consciousness means in practice. It means recognizing that your individual struggle is part of a collective struggle. It means building solidarity with other diaspora families, with Indigenous families, with all marginalized communities.

---

### SEGMENT 2: DIASPORA NETWORKS AND SOLIDARITY (12 minutes)

**SPEAKER 2:** So how are diaspora families building power? First, through networks. Diaspora families are connecting with each other. They're sharing stories. They're building community.

**SPEAKER 3:** There are organizations like Maison d'Haïti, BCHM, CETSO that are doing this work. They're creating spaces for diaspora families to gather, to organize, to build collective power.

**SPEAKER 1:** And there are individual activists—people like Jean Ernest Pierre, Tamara Thermitus, Gilbert Mirambeau—who are using their platforms to amplify diaspora voices, to demand accountability, to build movements.

**SPEAKER 4:** And there are faith-based communities. Haitian churches in Montreal, Laval, Ottawa, Gatineau are providing support, organizing, building community. They're centers of diaspora power.

**SPEAKER 2:** And now, with podcasts like this, with media platforms like Justice for Barran, diaspora families are telling their own stories. They're bypassing traditional media gatekeepers. They're reaching their communities directly.

**SPEAKER 3:** This is what international family solidarity looks like. It's diaspora families connecting across borders. It's Haitian families in Canada connecting with family in Haiti. It's Haitian families connecting with Indigenous families, with other marginalized communities.

**SPEAKER 1:** And it's powerful. When diaspora families organize, when they build collective power, they can demand systemic change. They can hold governments accountable. They can transform institutions.

---

### SEGMENT 3: LEGAL ACCOUNTABILITY AND SYSTEMIC CHANGE (12 minutes)

**SPEAKER 1:** Let's talk about what systemic change looks like. What does accountability mean? What does justice look like?

**SPEAKER 2:** First, it means reforming the DPJ. It means cultural competency training. It means hiring Creole-speaking social workers. It means stopping the criminalization of poverty. It means investigating why Haitian children are disproportionately in the child protection system.

**SPEAKER 4:** Second, it means reforming the medical system. It means Creole-speaking healthcare providers. It means culturally appropriate mental health services. It means affordable medications. It means recognizing diaspora trauma.

**SPEAKER 3:** Third, it means political accountability. It means elected officials responding to constituents. It means investigating the silence. It means holding politicians accountable for abandoning diaspora families.

**SPEAKER 1:** And fourth, it means legal precedent. Cases like Barran's can create legal precedent. They can establish that the DPJ violated rights. They can establish that the medical system failed. They can establish that politicians abandoned their constituents.

**SPEAKER 2:** And when legal precedent is established, it creates pressure for systemic change. Other families can use that precedent. Advocates can use that precedent. Movements can use that precedent.

**SPEAKER 3:** This is why Justice for Barran is so important. It's not just about one family. It's about creating legal precedent that protects all diaspora families.

**SPEAKER 4:** And it's about international accountability. When diaspora families build international movements, when they appeal to international human rights bodies, they create pressure for change at every level.

---

### SEGMENT 4: THE MCGOVERN ARTS INSTITUTE AS LEGACY (12 minutes)

**SPEAKER 2:** And then there's the McGovern Arts Institute Community Healing Centre. This is a vision for lasting legacy. This is about creating institutions that serve diaspora families for generations to come.

**SPEAKER 3:** The McGovern Arts Institute is rooted in the legacy of Joe McGovern—a man who was part of Haitian community, who understood the importance of cultural connection. The healing centre honors that legacy while serving contemporary diaspora families.

**SPEAKER 1:** And the healing centre is funded by donations from diaspora families, from allies, from people who believe in justice and healing. It's funded by people who recognize that diaspora families deserve support, deserve healing, deserve justice.

**SPEAKER 4:** And the healing centre provides services that diaspora families need—Creole language education, mental health services rooted in cultural healing, art programs, community support. It's about creating the conditions for diaspora families to thrive.

**SPEAKER 2:** But it's also about legacy. It's about creating an institution that will serve Haitian-Canadian families for decades to come. It's about ensuring that future generations grow up with connection to their culture, their history, their identity.

**SPEAKER 3:** And it's about international family legacy. The healing centre connects diaspora families in Canada with family in Haiti. It supports diaspora consciousness. It builds international solidarity.

**SPEAKER 1:** This is what building legacy means. It's not just about one family's case. It's about creating institutions that transform systems, that serve communities, that build collective power.

---

### SEGMENT 5: DIASPORA CONSCIOUSNESS AND INTERNATIONAL FAMILY IDENTITY (12 minutes)

**SPEAKER 3:** Let's talk about diaspora consciousness one more time. What does it mean to be part of an international family? What does it mean to maintain ties to Haiti while building a life in Canada?

**SPEAKER 2:** Diaspora consciousness means recognizing that you're part of multiple communities. You're Haitian. You're Canadian. You're part of an international family. You're part of a diaspora. All of these identities are valid. All of these identities are important.

**SPEAKER 1:** And diaspora consciousness means recognizing that your struggle is part of a larger struggle. When Haitian families are targeted by the DPJ, when they're silenced by politicians, when they're failed by medical systems—that's not just individual tragedy. That's systemic oppression. That's part of a larger pattern.

**SPEAKER 4:** And diaspora consciousness means building solidarity. When Haitian families connect with Indigenous families, when they connect with other marginalized communities, when they build international movements—that's diaspora consciousness in action.

**SPEAKER 3:** And it means maintaining cultural identity. It means speaking Creole. It means engaging with Haitian culture. It means teaching children about their heritage. It means maintaining ties to Haiti.

**SPEAKER 2:** This is what makes diaspora families powerful. They're not isolated. They're connected to international networks. They're connected to history. They're connected to resistance movements. They're part of something larger than themselves.

**SPEAKER 1:** And when diaspora families recognize that power, when they organize, when they demand accountability, when they build movements—they can transform systems. They can create justice. They can build legacy.
