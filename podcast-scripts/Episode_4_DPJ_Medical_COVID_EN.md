# Episode 4: DPJ + Medical System During COVID - Targeting Haitian Diaspora in Crisis
## English Script (4 Speakers, ~50 minutes)

**INTRO MUSIC:** Haitian drums and strings (15 seconds)

---

### SPEAKER INTRODUCTIONS

**SPEAKER 1 (Legal/Justice - Male):** Welcome to Episode 4: DPJ and Medical System Failures During COVID. I'm [Speaker 1], a legal advocate.

**SPEAKER 4 (Medical/Health - Female):** I'm [Speaker 4], a healthcare advocate working with marginalized communities.

**SPEAKER 2 (Community/Healing - Female):** I'm [Speaker 2], a community organizer.

**SPEAKER 3 (Diaspora/Advocacy - Male):** I'm [Speaker 3], a diaspora activist. Today we're exploring how the pandemic created a perfect storm for Haitian families in Canada.

---

### SEGMENT 1: THE PERFECT STORM (10 minutes)

**SPEAKER 3:** When COVID lockdowns began in March 2020, Haitian families in Laval and across Quebec faced an unprecedented crisis. Lockdowns meant isolation. Isolation meant no community support, no church, no extended family. For diaspora families, this was devastating.

**SPEAKER 2:** At the same time, many Haitian families lost their jobs or had their hours cut. They were locked down, isolated, and facing economic devastation. They couldn't send remittances to Haiti. They couldn't pay rent. They were in crisis.

**SPEAKER 1:** And here's what's critical: the DPJ didn't stop operating during COVID. In fact, lockdowns made families more vulnerable to DPJ intervention. More home visits. More opportunities to see "poverty" and open investigations. Haitian families were targeted during the most vulnerable period.

**SPEAKER 4:** From a medical perspective, COVID was catastrophic for Haitian communities. Healthcare access collapsed. Mental health services were unavailable. Medications were unaffordable. And Haitian patients faced racism in the healthcare system—doctors refusing to treat them, dismissing their symptoms, blaming their poverty for their health problems.

---

### SEGMENT 2: DPJ TARGETING DURING LOCKDOWNS (12 minutes)

**SPEAKER 1:** Let's talk about what happened to Haitian families during lockdowns. The DPJ was still operating. In fact, they had more access to families because of lockdowns.

**SPEAKER 2:** I worked with families who experienced this directly. A family loses their job during lockdowns. They can't pay rent. They're living in substandard housing. The DPJ gets called—by a neighbor, by a school, by someone—and they show up at the door. They see poverty. They open an investigation.

**SPEAKER 3:** And here's what's important: poverty is not abuse. Poverty is not neglect. But the DPJ treats it as if it is. They criminalize poverty. They remove children from families because of economic circumstances.

**SPEAKER 1:** During COVID, this became even more extreme. Families were locked down, isolated, unable to access support services. And the DPJ was using that isolation as grounds for intervention. "You're not accessing services. You're not getting your child mental health support." But the services weren't available!

**SPEAKER 4:** And the medical system was failing at the same time. A child needs mental health services. But mental health services are closed or unavailable. The family can't access care. And then the DPJ shows up and says "you're neglecting your child's mental health needs."

**SPEAKER 2:** This is systemic failure compounded by systemic failure. The medical system fails. The DPJ fails. And Haitian families are caught in the middle.

**SPEAKER 3:** And Haitian families were particularly targeted because of poverty. Haitian communities in Quebec have higher poverty rates. They're more likely to live in substandard housing. They're more likely to be unemployed or underemployed. So when the DPJ is looking for families to investigate, they look at poor neighborhoods. They look at Haitian communities.

---

### SEGMENT 3: MEDICAL SYSTEM COLLAPSE FOR HAITIAN COMMUNITIES (12 minutes)

**SPEAKER 4:** Let's talk about what happened to the medical system during COVID. Healthcare access collapsed for everyone, but it was particularly devastating for Haitian communities.

**SPEAKER 2:** Haitian patients were deprioritized. Haitian communities were seen as lower priority. Healthcare providers were overwhelmed, and they made choices about who to prioritize. Haitian patients were often deprioritized.

**SPEAKER 1:** And there was racism. Haitian patients faced discrimination in healthcare settings. Doctors making assumptions about Haitian patients based on stereotypes. Dismissing their symptoms. Blaming their poverty for their health problems.

**SPEAKER 4:** Mental health services were particularly devastated. Therapists were overwhelmed. Services were moved online, which created barriers for families without reliable internet. And culturally appropriate mental health services—services that understood Haitian trauma, migration trauma, systemic racism—were completely unavailable.

**SPEAKER 3:** And medications. Medications became unaffordable during COVID. Families were losing jobs. They couldn't afford prescriptions. And the healthcare system wasn't providing support. Haitian families were going without medications they needed.

**SPEAKER 2:** I worked with families dealing with this. A child has a mental health condition. They need medication. The family can't afford it. The healthcare system doesn't provide support. The child's condition gets worse. And then the DPJ shows up.

**SPEAKER 1:** This is how systems fail diaspora families. The medical system fails. The DPJ fails. And Haitian families are blamed for systemic failures.

**SPEAKER 4:** And here's what's important: Haitian communities have specific health needs. They have higher rates of certain conditions. They have migration trauma. They have systemic racism trauma. They need culturally appropriate healthcare. But that care was completely unavailable during COVID.

---

### SEGMENT 4: THE REMITTANCE CRISIS (8 minutes)

**SPEAKER 3:** And we can't talk about Haitian families during COVID without talking about the remittance crisis. Remittances are how diaspora families support family in Haiti. They're not charity; they're survival.

**SPEAKER 2:** During COVID, many Haitian families in Canada lost their jobs or had their hours cut. They couldn't send remittances to Haiti. And families in Haiti were starving. There was a humanitarian crisis in Haiti, and diaspora families couldn't help.

**SPEAKER 1:** This created psychological trauma for diaspora families. You're locked down in Canada, isolated from your community, losing your income, facing DPJ investigation. And your mother is starving in Haiti. You can't help. You can't do anything. The helplessness is overwhelming.

**SPEAKER 3:** And this is where diaspora consciousness becomes critical. Diaspora families are maintaining ties to Haiti. They're sending remittances. They're supporting family. But when they can't do that—when COVID prevents them from supporting family—it creates a crisis of identity and purpose.

**SPEAKER 4:** And from a mental health perspective, this is devastating. Diaspora families are dealing with migration trauma, systemic racism, economic crisis, and now they can't support family in Haiti. The mental health impact is severe.

---

### SEGMENT 5: INTERSECTIONAL TARGETING (8 minutes)

**SPEAKER 1:** So let's put this together. During COVID, Haitian families in Canada were facing:
- Economic devastation (job loss, reduced hours)
- Isolation (lockdowns, no community support)
- Medical system collapse (healthcare unavailable)
- DPJ targeting (increased investigations)
- Remittance crisis (can't support family in Haiti)
- Mental health crisis (no services available)

**SPEAKER 2:** This is intersectional oppression. It's not one problem; it's multiple systems failing simultaneously. And Haitian families, already marginalized, were hit hardest.

**SPEAKER 3:** And this is where the international family dimension becomes critical. Haitian families are not just dealing with Canadian systems. They're also dealing with the crisis in Haiti. They're caught between two crises. They're trying to survive in Canada while family is starving in Haiti.

**SPEAKER 4:** And the healthcare system wasn't recognizing this. Doctors weren't asking about family in Haiti. They weren't understanding the psychological impact of diaspora crisis. They were treating Haitian patients as if they were isolated individuals, not as members of international families.

**SPEAKER 1:** This is systemic failure at every level. The Canadian system failed. The Haitian system failed. And diaspora families were caught in the middle.

---

### SEGMENT 6: WHAT ACCOUNTABILITY LOOKS LIKE (5 minutes)

**SPEAKER 2:** So what does accountability look like? How do we transform systems so that Haitian families are not targeted during crisis?

**SPEAKER 1:** First, we need to stop criminalizing poverty. We need to recognize that economic hardship is not abuse or neglect. We need to provide support, not punishment.

**SPEAKER 4:** Second, we need to reform the medical system. We need Creole-speaking healthcare providers. We need culturally appropriate mental health services. We need to recognize diaspora trauma and international family needs.

**SPEAKER 3:** Third, we need to support diaspora families. We need to recognize their role in supporting family in Haiti. We need to provide support during crises like COVID. We need to recognize that diaspora families are part of international systems.

**SPEAKER 2:** And finally, we need to hold systems accountable. We need investigations into DPJ practices. We need investigations into healthcare discrimination. We need to transform systems so that Haitian families are protected, not targeted.

---
