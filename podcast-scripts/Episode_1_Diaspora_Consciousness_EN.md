# Episode 1: Diaspora Consciousness - International Family Justice
## English Script (3 Speakers, ~45 minutes)

**INTRO MUSIC:** Traditional Haitian drums and strings (15 seconds)

---

### SPEAKER INTRODUCTIONS

**SPEAKER 1 (Legal/Justice - Male):** Welcome to Justice for Barran, a podcast series exploring systemic failures, government accountability, and the power of international family solidarity. I'm [Speaker 1], a legal advocate focused on systemic justice and human rights.

**SPEAKER 2 (Community/Healing - Female):** I'm [Speaker 2], a community organizer working with diaspora families navigating child protection systems and institutional barriers.

**SPEAKER 3 (Diaspora/Advocacy - Male):** I'm [Speaker 3], a diaspora activist and organizer focused on connecting Haitian communities across borders and building collective power.

**SPEAKER 1:** Today, we're discussing diaspora consciousness—what it means to be Haitian, to be part of an international family, and how systemic failures affect diaspora communities across Canada, the United States, and Haiti itself.

---

### SEGMENT 1: WHAT IS DIASPORA CONSCIOUSNESS? (8 minutes)

**SPEAKER 3:** Diaspora consciousness is about maintaining emotional, cultural, and economic ties to Haiti while navigating life in a new country. It's about being caught between worlds—loving Haiti, supporting family there, while also building a life here in Canada. It's not one or the other; it's both, always.

**SPEAKER 2:** And for families like Barran's, diaspora consciousness becomes even more complex. You're not just Haitian; you're Haitian-Irish, Haitian-Indigenous. You're navigating multiple identities, multiple systems of oppression, multiple languages. Your children are growing up with this consciousness—they're part of an international family that spans continents.

**SPEAKER 1:** The challenge is that Canadian systems—the DPJ, the medical system, the legal system—don't recognize or respect diaspora consciousness. They see "poor Haitian family" and apply their own narrow lens. They don't see the strength, the resilience, the international support networks that diaspora families maintain.

**SPEAKER 3:** Exactly. And that's where the problem starts. When you come to Canada seeking a better life, you're told "this is better than Haiti." But then you face the same systemic racism, the same poverty discrimination, the same institutional failures. And you're isolated—your extended family is in Haiti, your support network is fragmented across borders.

**SPEAKER 2:** During COVID lockdowns, this became even more visible. Families in Laval couldn't support their extended families in Haiti. They couldn't visit elderly parents. They couldn't send remittances. They were locked down, isolated, and then targeted by the DPJ because of poverty. The system weaponized diaspora vulnerability.

---

### SEGMENT 2: BARRAN'S STORY - INTRODUCTION (10 minutes)

**SPEAKER 1:** Let's talk about Barran's story specifically. This is a case that exemplifies how diaspora families are failed by Canadian institutions. Barran is a young person from a Haitian-Irish-Indigenous family. Since 2021, his family sought help after serious trauma—a teenager stabbed three times, major impacts, mental health destroyed.

**SPEAKER 2:** The Direction de la Protection de la Jeunesse—the DPJ—became involved. But here's what happened: they withdrew in January 2023 with no adequate follow-up, no education support, no mental health services. And the whole time, Jordan's Principle—which guarantees no-delay services for First Nations children—was completely ignored.

**SPEAKER 3:** This is the pattern we see again and again with diaspora families. The system gets involved, creates more trauma, then abandons you. You're left with nothing—no support, no answers, no justice. And if you're Haitian, if you're poor, if you're trying to navigate systems in a language that's not your first language, you're even more vulnerable.

**SPEAKER 1:** What's particularly striking about Barran's case is that it's not unique. This is happening to hundreds, thousands of Haitian families across Quebec and Canada. The DPJ is targeting poor families. Poverty becomes grounds for child removal. And diaspora families, already isolated and vulnerable, are hit hardest.

**SPEAKER 2:** And the family tried to get help. They contacted elected officials at every level—municipal, provincial, federal. More than 50 elected representatives. Do you know how many responded? Zero. The silence is deafening. It's not accidental; it's systemic.

---

### SEGMENT 3: DIASPORA FAMILIES IN CANADA - THE HIDDEN CRISIS (12 minutes)

**SPEAKER 3:** When we talk about diaspora consciousness, we need to talk about the reality of diaspora families in Canada. Millions of Haitians and their descendants live outside Haiti—in the US, Canada, Dominican Republic. They maintain strong ties to Haiti while navigating integration in their host countries. But integration doesn't mean acceptance. It doesn't mean safety.

**SPEAKER 1:** In Quebec specifically, Haitian families face compounded discrimination. There's a long history of anti-Haitian sentiment in Quebec. Haitian immigrants are stereotyped, marginalized, criminalized. And when you're poor—which many Haitian families are—you become a target for the DPJ.

**SPEAKER 2:** I've worked with dozens of Haitian families. The stories are heartbreaking and consistent. A child gets sick; the parent can't afford medication; DPJ gets called for "neglect." A family lives in substandard housing because they can't afford better; DPJ gets called for "unsafe conditions." A parent is working multiple jobs to send money to Haiti; DPJ gets called because the child is unsupervised. Poverty is criminalized.

**SPEAKER 3:** And the medical system compounds this. Haitian families face racism in healthcare. Doctors don't understand Haitian health needs. Medications are unaffordable. Mental health services are culturally inappropriate. During COVID, healthcare access completely collapsed for Haitian communities. And no one was talking about it.

**SPEAKER 1:** This is what diaspora consciousness means in practice. It means you're supporting family in Haiti—sending remittances, maintaining emotional ties—while simultaneously being criminalized and abandoned in Canada. It means your children are growing up with this contradiction: "We're told Canada is better, but we're being failed by Canadian systems."

---

### SEGMENT 4: INTERNATIONAL FAMILY SOLIDARITY (10 minutes)

**SPEAKER 2:** But here's what's powerful: diaspora families are also building international solidarity. They're connecting across borders. They're recognizing that Haitian families in Canada, Haitian families in the US, families in Haiti—they're all facing similar systemic failures. They're all being targeted by institutions that don't value their lives.

**SPEAKER 3:** And that's where Barran's story becomes more than just one family's tragedy. It becomes a catalyst for international family solidarity. When we talk about Justice for Barran, we're talking about justice for all diaspora families. We're talking about recognizing that systemic racism, poverty discrimination, and institutional failure are international problems.

**SPEAKER 1:** The Haitian diaspora has always been organized, politically conscious. Think about the Haitian Revolution—diaspora Haitians were central to that struggle. Think about the civil rights movements in the US—Haitian-Americans were there. Think about contemporary activism—diaspora Haitians are leading movements for justice and accountability.

**SPEAKER 2:** And now, with Barran's case, we're seeing a new wave of diaspora consciousness. Haitian families in Quebec are connecting with Indigenous communities. They're connecting with other marginalized families. They're recognizing that their struggle is part of a larger struggle for justice, for dignity, for recognition.

**SPEAKER 3:** This is what international family solidarity means. It means Haitian families in Laval supporting Indigenous families in Quebec. It means families in Canada supporting family in Haiti. It means recognizing that we're all part of one international family fighting against systemic oppression.

---

### SEGMENT 5: CALL TO ACTION (5 minutes)

**SPEAKER 1:** So what does this mean? What's the call to action? First, we need to recognize diaspora consciousness as legitimate. We need to recognize that diaspora families have the right to maintain ties to their home countries while also building lives in Canada. That's not a threat; that's a strength.

**SPEAKER 2:** Second, we need to reform the DPJ. We need cultural competency training. We need Creole-speaking social workers. We need to stop criminalizing poverty. We need to recognize that different cultures have different parenting practices, and those practices are not abuse.

**SPEAKER 3:** Third, we need to amplify diaspora voices. We need Haitian families telling their own stories, in their own languages. We need Creole-language services, Creole-language advocacy, Creole-language media. This podcast is part of that—it's about making sure Haitian families hear this message in their own language.

**SPEAKER 1:** And finally, we need international family solidarity. We need Haitian families connecting with Indigenous families, with other marginalized communities. We need to recognize that our struggles are interconnected, and our power is collective.

---

### OUTRO MUSIC:** Haitian drums and strings (15 seconds)

**SPEAKER 2:** Thank you for listening to Episode 1: Diaspora Consciousness. Next episode: Barran's Story and Other Haitian Children in Canada. We'll explore specific cases, systemic patterns, and what justice looks like.

**SPEAKER 3:** This is Justice for Barran. Building international family solidarity. One story at a time.
